# When Christianity Was Adopted as the Official Religion

Christianity became the official religion of the Roman Empire through the Edict of Thessalonica, issued on February 27, AD 380, by Emperor Theodosius I, along with co-emperors Gratian and Valentinian II.

## Historical Context

Following Constantine's Edict of Milan in 313 AD, which granted religious toleration, Christianity was allowed but not yet the official religion. Internal disputes among Christians persisted, particularly between Nicene Christians and Arians.

Theodosius I, a devout Nicene Christian, sought to unify the empire under one orthodox faith after Arianism had dominated the Eastern part of the empire.

## The Edict of Thessalonica

The edict established Nicene Christianity as the state church and condemned other Christian creeds, such as Arianism, as heresies punishable by law.

Key provisions:
- All subjects were required to profess the faith delivered by Apostle Peter to the Romans.
- The faith was defined by Pope Damasus I of Rome and Peter of Alexandria.
- Belief in the Trinity (Father, Son, Holy Spirit) of equal majesty.
- Heretics were barred from calling their gatherings churches and faced divine and imperial punishment.

## Impact and Significance

This edict marked the end of religious toleration in the Roman Empire and opened the door for repression against dissenters. It was incorporated into the Theodosian Code (Book XVI) and reinforced by the First Council of Constantinople in 381 AD, which finalized the Nicene Creed.

This was the first secular law defining Christian orthodoxy for the Roman state, shifting from mere toleration to active enforcement and accelerating the Christianization of the empire.
